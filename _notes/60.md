I read and very much enjoyed Ursula K Le Guin's short essay *The Carrier Bag Theory of Fiction*. I wasn't aware of the piece, but saw it in [book form](https://www.ursulakleguin.com/the-carrier-bag-theory-of-fiction) at Dog Eared Books in SF when I was visiting recently, with an intro by Donna Haraway. With both Le Guin and Haraway there, I felt compelled to pick it up. The piece is quite short, entertaining, and memorable -- on the themes of technology, and gender, and how we think about narrative/storytelling. In this post I document some notes/memories from the piece.

* The bit referencing *2001* I found very funny and made me laugh aloud, as someone who watched that a number of times when I was younger.
* On the narrative front, a key idea is that of the *hero* and the tools of the hero (the spear, the sword), and the kind of narrative that goes along with them. The hero who goes out, fights or overcomes some obstacle, and then returns. The linearity and directedness of this "hero's journey".
* And yet in the way that Le Guin alludes, the place of gathering and holding is in a way fundamentally prior to the work of the hero or his tools; if energy is being forced outward, to where is it returning? The hero's journey requires a home that the hero returns to. If you are going to go out and kill a big animal, how is it that you will transport the winnings home?
  > with or before the tool that forces energy outward, we made the tool that brings energy home. It makes sense to me.
* One note I have is that Le Guin is happy to accept that the hero story is an easy one to tell, to make interesting, exciting. And that the story of gathering is harder ("It is hard to tell a really gripping tale of how I wrested a wild-oat seed from its husk"), even if it's the kind of story that Le Guin prefers. She writes:
  > I said it was hard to make a gripping tale of how we wrested the wild oats from their husks, I didn't say it was impossible.
* In another memorable moment of the essay, Le Guin discusses a glossary of Virginia Woolf in which Woolf defines *heroism* as 'botulism', and defines *hero* as 'bottle'. Le Guin writes:
  > The hero as bottle, a stringent re-evaluation. I now propose the bottle as hero.
* [Botulism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botulism) is a kind of toxic infection, often caused by improper food preparation. Poison, toxic.
  > It is the story that makes the difference. It is the story that hid my hummanity from me, the story the mammoth hunters tolda bout bashing, thrusting, raping, killing, about the Hero. The wonderful, poisonous story of Botulism. The killer story.
* Le Guin discusses how the Hero story is fundamentally about Man in the sense of gendered maleness. If making and killing with a weapon is what it meant to be human then "I was either extemely defective as a human being, or not human at all".
* A lot about *linearity* in tools and narrative. Starting *here* and going *there* as being a key aspect of the heroic narrative form. A feature of the killing spear too: being thrown, hitting and killing your target.
* On the connection between narrative and technology and science fiction. Technology making has always been tied up with narrative and future-imagining, science fiction. And so it makes sense that Le Guin treats the reshaping of these things together.
  > That is why I like novels: instead of heroes they have people in them. ... So, when i Came to write science-fiction novels, I came lugging htis great heavy sack of stuff, my carrier bag full of wimps and klutzes, ... full of beginnings wihtout ends, of initiations, of losses, of transformations and translations, and far more tricks than conflicts, far fewer triumphs than snares and delusions; full of space ships that get stuck, missions that fail and people who don't understand.

The hero and his narrative is an idealization, an imagination, a myth. It must fit in a particular box, a particular form. Real people and real lives of course don't have that most of the time.
