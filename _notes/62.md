From Claude, I came across this idea of *conservationism* or perhaps *conservatism* as being about valuing *particular* things, and your relation to them vs some notion of relationally-independent general value. Here is Claude's summary:

> The cleanest philosophical statement of it comes, ironically, from a Marxist. G.A. Cohen's late essay "Rescuing Conservatism" distinguishes valuing something as a repository of value — in which case you should trade up whenever something more valuable comes along — from valuing it as the particular thing it is, with its history and your relation to it. Cohen's small-c conservatism says some things warrant the second attitude, and that a pure maximizing rationality is therefore blind to a whole category of value. This is exactly the axis I was pointing at: economics is the repository view formalized (opportunity cost just is the injunction to trade up), while mending this pair of jeans — rather than acquiring equivalent-or-better jeans at lower cost — is Cohen's particularism enacted in fabric. The jeans are irreplaceable not because no substitute exists but because substitution is the wrong category.

I was interested to read the Cohen essay, but could only find an earlier [working paper version](https://jablevine.com/papers/cohen_conservatism_2004.pdf) for free online. A published version, I think, is [here](https://academic.oup.com/book/4117/chapter-abstract/145857931?redirectedFrom=fulltext#no-access-message). 

Still, the working paper version seems to get at some of the key ideas. In the paper, Cohen discusses two elements of conservatism, one of which is the above idea of "particular valuing". Here is how Cohen describes this in Section 3 of the paper: 

> The conservative attachment that I endorse is to *particular* things, as such, which means that the attachment is not to the external purposes that the things serve, or to the principles that they exemplify, which might be better served or exemplified by other things, by which we would nevertheless not replace the things to which we are attached.

He expands:

> The conservative impulse is to conserve what is valuable, that is, *the particular things that are valuable*. A salient, though not the only, alternative to conserving what is valuable is to maximize value, but clear-thinking conservatives are resolved to conserve the valuable at the expense of maximizing value: *what we distinctively value are the particular bearers of value*. A commitment to the conservation of what has value is at the centre of the specific conservative attitude that I am seeking to describe.

One way to frame this, I think, is as emphasizing the purely non-instrumental aspects of valuing. What is valued is the *particular* bearer of value for its own sake and particularity -- not because it provides or enables some other thing. It's interesting to try to deeply internalize this distinction. A thought experiment that roughly aligns with some of Cohen's discussion: think of some thing in your life that has some sort of instrumental value. For example, I value this pair of pants because it is comfortable, or looks good on me, or has a nice color. Now imagine that that pair of pants is destroyed and replaced by a *new* pair of pants that is the same (or perhaps even better) on all those instrumental dimensions of value. Is there something lost in that act of replacement (and its implied destruction)? And if so, why?

Cohen discusses how this is different from, say, the perspective of the utilitarian: 

> Utilitarians, for example, purport to see nothing wrong with destroying value, if more value results. To seek to maximize value is to see nothing wrong in the destruction of value, as opposed to in a reduction in the total amount of value. Unlike the conservative, the utilitarian is indifferent between adding to what we’ve now got, at no cost, something that has five units of value, and adding something worth ten units of value at the expense of destroying something worth five. The utilitarian says: “Let’s have as much value as possible, regardless of what happens, as a result of that policy, to existing bearers of value: they don’t matter, as such.” Conservatism sets itself against that maximizing attitude, according to which the things that possess value, by contrast with the value they possess, don’t matter at all.

Cohen agrees that:

> Conservatism is an expensive taste, because conservatives sacrifice value in order not to sacrifice things that have value.

This framing is interesting to reflect on. For me it brings up the way in which this idea is about rejecting the abstraction of values, kind of like how Nietzsche rejects Plato's idealism. 


but also highlights that conservatism in his sense does not have problem with "the new" per se -- only when it involves the need to destroy the existing holders of value to make its new way: 

> The conservative propensity is to conserve, to not destroy, and, therefore, to not replace, even (within limits) by something more valuable. [...] There is no conservative objection, in my sense of conservatism, to things of new kinds, as long as they leave the old kinds of things intact.

Cohen goes on to explain that also, being opposed to the destruction of exiting holders of value does not imply *not* valuing what is new:

> When conservatives lament what has been lost, say, for example, traditional craftsmanship, when they lament, William-Morris-like, that now everything is made by machines, then some anti-conservatives scornfully reply that the world we have lost was a world of poverty and toil, and that conservatives are irresponsible romantics. But the fact, and I agree that it is one, that humankind is a net beneficiary of modernization, is not a reason for not lamenting what has been lost, unless you’re a non-conservative evaluator. *A conservative can believe that what rises from the ashes is the greatest building ever and that it was right to build it, yet still feel distraught that the old building was destroyed.*

In the piece, Cohen distinguishes *particular value*--valuing per se those objects while are extant holders of value--from the notion of *personal value*. Personal value is more about valuing something because of its relational history to oneself. He discusses a pencil eraser that he has had for a long time, and explains:

> I would hate to lose this eraser. I would hate that even if I knew it could be readily replaced, not only, if I so wished, by a pristine square one, but even by one of precisely the off-round shape and the same dingy colour that mine has now acquired. There is no feature that stands apart from its history that makes me want to keep this eraser. I want *my* eraser, with *its* history. What could be more human than that?

Another interesting discussion: 

> Consider the resistance to suburban supermarketization, on behalf of neighbourhood shops. People who seek to protect neighbourhood shops point out their many advantages, the many purposes that they serve so well: local effects like their accessibility to old and infirm people, a meeting place that stimulates community, eccentric product lines, and also macro-effects like reducing motor traffic, and better life-chances for people from deprived minorities who get an opportunity to rise on the social scale, through self-, and even other-, employment.

> But we deceive ourselves if we think that it is only because they deliver specifiable economic and social benefits that we cherish our local shops. It is not only the purposes they serve that justify our resistance to their destruction. It is also because in all their vagariously caused uniqueness they are part of a social and cultural landscape to which we belong. 

This type of thing reminds me more of MacIntyre and other sorts of defenses of partialism which I dredged up in my own thinking most recently [here](https://jeffreyfossett.com/2021/11/15/partiality-and-carbon-chauvinism.html). 

Another nice excerpt: 

> We are attached to particular things because we need to belong to something, and we therefore need some things to belong to us. We can’t belong to something abstract. We do not keep the cathedrals just because they’re beautiful, but also because they are part of our past. We want the past to be present among us.
